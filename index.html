Guidance  /  Windows

Featured guide · Windows

Repair or replace? Making the right decision for an original timber window.

How to assess decay, failed joints, glazing, draughts and historic significance before deciding whether careful repair or sympathetic replacement is appropriate.

HJ Written in the workshop by Harry Jackson 8 minute read Last reviewed August 2026

Almost every enquiry we take about a rotten window starts with the same sentence: "I think they're past saving." Usually they aren't. Timber windows were built to be repaired, and the ones still in place after a hundred and fifty years are proof that repair works.

That said, there is a point at which repair stops being sensible — and knowing where that point sits is the difference between spending money well and spending it twice. This guide sets out how we assess a window on site, so you can make the same judgement before you ring anyone.

Start with what has actually failed

Decay in a timber window is almost never evenly spread. Water collects at the bottom, so that is where the damage is. The parts that fail first, in roughly this order, are the bottom rail of the lower sash, the cill, the bottom few inches of the stiles, and the putty line on the lower panes.

Everything above the meeting rail is usually sound. That matters, because it means the majority of the joinery — the parts that are hardest and most expensive to reproduce — is often perfectly serviceable.

The screwdriver test

Push a screwdriver gently into the timber at the bottom corners of the sash and along the cill. Sound timber resists. Decayed timber gives way like cork and stays soft an inch or more in. Do it in three or four places on each window rather than one, because a single soft spot near a joint tells you very little on its own.

Five things that point toward repair

  • The decay is localised. Soft timber confined to the bottom rail, cill or the lowest few inches of the stiles can be cut out and spliced with new material.
  • The joints are still tight. Mortice and tenon joints that have not opened up mean the frame still has structural integrity.
  • The glass is original. Cylinder or crown glass with visible distortion is genuinely irreplaceable and adds real value to a period elevation.
  • The property is listed or in a conservation area. Repair is nearly always the route a conservation officer will prefer, and often the only one they will approve.
  • Only one or two windows are affected. Repairing three windows is straightforward. Repairing eighteen becomes a false economy against a full replacement programme.
Good to know

A rotten bottom rail is one of the most common reasons people are told a window is beyond saving. It is also one of the most straightforward repairs we do — the rail is cut out and a new section scarfed in, usually without removing the sash from the building for more than a day.

Five things that point toward replacement

  • The joints have opened. Once the mortice and tenon joints have failed and the sash is racking out of square, you are rebuilding the window rather than repairing it.
  • Decay has reached the frame and box. Sash boxes built into the masonry are far harder to repair in situ, and rot there often indicates a longer-standing water problem.
  • The timber is modern softwood in poor condition. Windows replaced in the 1970s and 80s were often made from fast-grown softwood that does not repair well and will fail again.
  • Previous repairs have used filler. Large areas of two-part filler trap moisture behind them and accelerate decay in the surrounding timber.
  • You need a meaningful thermal improvement. Repair keeps single glazing. If the goal is comfort and running costs, a sympathetic replacement with slimline units may be the honest answer.
The question is rarely "can this be repaired?" — nearly anything can. It is whether repairing it is the better use of the money.

What repair actually costs against replacement

As a rough working rule, a competent repair to a single sash window — splicing the bottom rail, renewing the cill, re-cording, re-puttying and redecorating — costs somewhere between a third and a half of a like-for-like replacement. That ratio holds until the joints fail, at which point repair costs climb steeply and the gap closes.

Where people lose money is repairing a window twice. If the underlying cause of the decay — a failed gutter, a cracked cill, a blocked drainage groove — is not fixed at the same time, the repair will fail on the same timescale as the original.

Historic significance, and why it changes the answer

On a listed building, the windows are part of what is listed. The test a conservation officer applies is not "is this window in good condition?" but "does this window contribute to the significance of the building?" An original Georgian sash with crown glass and hand-cut joinery contributes a great deal, even in poor repair. A 1980s softwood replacement contributes nothing, and replacing it sympathetically may actively improve the building.

That distinction is why two windows in identical physical condition can get completely different answers. If you are unsure which category you are in, our three-question consent checker will tell you where you stand before you commit to anything.

What to do next

Photograph the window from far enough back to see the whole opening and the brickwork around it, then take close-ups of the bottom rail, the cill and any joint that looks open. Do the screwdriver test in four places and note where it goes in. That is enough for us to give you a sensible view before anyone comes out.

We will tell you honestly if repair is the better option — including on jobs where replacement would earn us more. A window we repair well is a window someone recommends us for.

Guidance  /  Conservation

Guide · Conservation

Replacing windows in a conservation area.

What to consider before changing joinery, from matching existing details to the conversation with your local planning department — and why most applications fail on detail rather than principle.

HJ Written in the workshop by Harry Jackson 9 minute read Last reviewed August 2026

A conservation area is not a listed building, and the two get confused constantly. Being in one does not mean you cannot change your windows. It means the council has a say in how the street looks, and that changes what you can do rather than whether you can do anything at all.

Conservation area, listed building, Article 4 — the difference

These three things are separate and can overlap. Getting them straight is the single most useful thing you can do before spending any money.

  • Conservation area. A designated area where the character of the place is protected. Ordinary houses inside one usually keep their permitted development rights for windows, but the council can restrict changes that harm the street's appearance.
  • Listed building. The building itself is protected, inside and out. Listed building consent is normally required for window work even where it is like-for-like.
  • Article 4 direction. A specific removal of permitted development rights for a defined area — often covering windows and doors on the front elevation. If one applies to your street, you need planning permission for work that would otherwise be automatic.

Both Telford & Wrekin and Shropshire Council publish conservation area maps online, and Historic England's National Heritage List will tell you in under a minute whether a property is listed. If you would rather not wade through it, our consent checker asks three questions and gives you a plain answer.

What the council is actually protecting

It helps to understand what a conservation officer is looking at, because it is rarely the individual window. It is the street.

Period terraces read as a whole. When every house has the same glazing bar pattern, the same sightlines and the same painted timber, the eye reads a coherent frontage. Replace one house's windows with something chunkier, flatter or in a different material, and that house pulls out of the row. That is the harm the designation exists to prevent.

Nobody is trying to stop you having warm, working windows. They are trying to stop the street losing what makes it worth living in.

The four details that decide an application

In our experience applications very rarely fail because the principle of replacement was refused. They fail on detail. These are the four that matter most.

1. Sightlines

The width of the visible timber between panes of glass. Original sashes have slim sightlines; many modern replacements do not, because standard double-glazed units force thicker sections. A window that is correct in every other respect will still look wrong if the sightlines are heavy.

2. Glazing bar profile and width

Not just how many bars, but their section. An ovolo, a lambs tongue and a slim putty line all cast different shadows. Applied bars stuck to the face of a sealed unit are usually rejected on front elevations, because they read flat in daylight.

3. Reveal depth

How far the window sits back from the face of the brickwork. Period windows are recessed; sitting a replacement flush with the outer face changes the shadow line across the whole elevation and is one of the most commonly cited reasons for refusal.

4. Opening method

A sliding sash replaced with a top-hung casement changes the character of the opening even if the outline is identical. If the original was a sash, the replacement should slide.

Good to know

Slimline double-glazed units — typically a narrow cavity with a warm-edge spacer — exist specifically to solve the sightline problem. They are accepted in many conservation areas where standard units are not, and they are what we specify on most sensitive work.

Having the conversation early

Most councils offer pre-application advice, and on window work it is worth taking. A short conversation before drawings are produced is far cheaper than a refusal after. Take photographs of the existing windows, a note of what you propose to change, and — if you have them — section drawings showing the profile you intend to use.

We are happy to speak to the conservation officer directly, and often do. Officers are generally more receptive to a joiner explaining how a moulding will be run than to a homeowner relaying it second-hand.

What we provide for an application

  • Measured details of the existing windows, including section sizes and glazing bar widths
  • Joinery drawings of the proposed replacement at a scale the officer can assess
  • Timber specification, including species, finish and guarantee
  • Glazing specification with unit build-up and sightline dimensions
  • Where relevant, a written case for replacement over repair

A realistic timescale

Allow eight to twelve weeks from first conversation to a decision, longer if the application goes to committee. Manufacturing then follows, so a conservation area project realistically runs four to six months end to end. Starting the planning conversation early costs nothing and removes the part of the programme you cannot control.

This guide is general and is not a planning decision. Both councils in our area take a view case by case, and the right answer for your property depends on its specific context.

Accreditation

An approved Accoya manufacturer — and what that actually means.

Plenty of joiners will use Accoya. Being an approved manufacturer is a different thing, and it's the reason the guarantee on our external joinery holds.

The difference between using it and being approved to make with it.

Accoya is sustainably grown softwood put through acetylation — a process that chemically alters the structure of the wood so it can no longer take up water the way untreated timber does. It stops swelling, it stops shrinking, and it stops rotting.

The 50-year guarantee that comes with it isn't automatic. It depends on the material being bought through the approved supply chain, machined to the correct tolerances, sealed on every face and end grain, and finished to a specified standard. A workshop that isn't set up for it can void the guarantee without ever knowing.

We're an approved manufacturer, which means our process has been assessed against that standard. It's the single strongest credential we hold, and until now it's been a sentence buried halfway down a page.

How we work on protected buildings

What a conservation officer wants to see.

Consent applications fail on detail, not on principle. These are the four things that decide it.

  1. Evidence of what's there nowPhotographs and measured details of the existing windows, including section sizes and glazing bar widths.
  2. A like-for-like drawn detailJoinery drawings showing the replacement matches the original profile, not an approximation of it.
  3. A justified case for replacement over repairOfficers will nearly always prefer repair. Where a frame genuinely can't be saved, that has to be shown rather than asserted.
  4. The right glassSlimline units or single glazing where the sightline can't take a standard unit. This is where most applications come unstuck.
